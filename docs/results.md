# Results (draft, fifth pass — extended to all seven of Westminster's neighbouring boroughs)

> **See also: [`docs/ucl_benchmark_results.md`](ucl_benchmark_results.md)** —
> this document covers the project's *own* research questions
> (GAT-vs-XGBoost, conformal calibration, spatial vs temporal
> generalisation) on the annual pipeline. The **UCL/Gao et al. AccHR@20
> benchmark** is a separate, much larger body of work on the daily
> multi-step pipeline, with its own complete results compendium in that
> file. Headline from there (2026-09-02): using the real Ordnance Survey
> road network gives a statistically significant improvement
> (p=0.0010, n=18 windows, 3 boroughs), taking Westminster to 70.03% vs
> the reference paper's 68.98%.

*Addresses the "Results" chapter shape recommended for this degree: answer
each research question in order, report the primary comparison with
ablations, and interpret honestly. All numbers below come directly from
`reports/{borough}/baseline_vs_xgboost_results.csv` and
`reports/{borough}/conformal_calibration.csv`, generated by
`scripts/run_pipeline.py <Borough>` and freshly re-run/verified on
2026-08-31.*

**Important**: an earlier version of this document reported a GAT+GRU
model dramatically beating XGBoost (temporal PR-AUC 0.424 vs 0.332). That
was found to be invalid — a data-leakage bug where the model was trained
directly against the exact label it was later evaluated on (an in-sample
fit, not a held-out test). It was found, diagnosed, fixed with a proper
walk-forward training scheme. **Full account of the bug, how it was caught
(a suspiciously perfect 2000-epoch result), and the fix:
`docs/decision_log.md`, 2026-08-31 "CORRECTION" entry.** A second,
smaller instance of the same class of error was caught during this pass
too: a later log entry had kept reporting the same invalid 0.424/0.421
figures as if they were current, alongside a correctly-fixed 0.331/0.291
in a different entry — resolved by re-running Westminster and Lambeth
fresh rather than trusting either document (see `docs/decision_log.md`'s
"process note", same date). Every number in this document is from that
verified state. This is not a footnote — it materially changes the
project's headline finding, from "the graph decisively wins" to "XGBoost
is the safer default for same-segment prediction, but the graph
architecture gives a real, moderate improvement specifically when
generalising to unseen road segments, in most (not all) boroughs tested."

### 2.3 The architecture fix — real on Westminster, does not replicate on Lambeth

The naive GAT+GRU (4 attention heads, no residual connection) was
consistently beaten by its own "remove the graph" ablation. Diagnosis:
over-smoothing (a segment's own signal diluted by averaging with ~7-8
line-graph neighbours). Fix: 1 attention head + a residual/skip
connection. **Corrected ablation comparison** (temporal split,
walk-forward evaluation):

| model | Westminster PR-AUC | Lambeth PR-AUC |
|---|---:|---:|
| gat_temporal (full: fixed architecture) | **0.333** | 0.287 |
| gat_no_graph (fix minus the graph) | 0.307 | 0.291 |
| gat_no_temporal (fix minus the GRU) | 0.249 | 0.265 |

**Westminster**: the full model beats both ablations — the fix is real
here. **Lambeth**: the full model and `gat_no_graph` are close enough
(0.287 vs 0.291) to be statistically indistinguishable from a single run —
the graph component's contribution does not clearly replicate on this
borough.

### 2.4 Cross-borough validation — Camden, Kensington & Chelsea, Brent, Wandsworth, City of London

These five boroughs (Westminster's remaining real neighbours) were run to
find out whether the Westminster/Lambeth pattern was a genuine signal or a
two-borough coincidence. Selected rows (full detail in
`docs/scaling_to_london.md`):

| Borough | segments | temporal GAT vs XGBoost | spatiotemporal GAT vs XGBoost |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Camden | 5,486 | tie (0.291 vs 0.293) | GAT wins (0.319 vs 0.257) |
| Kensington & Chelsea | 4,581 | XGBoost wins (0.259 vs 0.283) | GAT wins (0.302 vs 0.251) |
| Brent | 9,007 | XGBoost wins (0.261 vs 0.275) | GAT wins (0.307 vs 0.270) |
| Wandsworth | 9,505 | GAT wins, slightly (0.259 vs 0.249) | GAT wins (0.246 vs 0.207) |
| City of London | 1,387 | XGBoost wins by a large margin (0.182 vs 0.400) | GAT wins (0.257 vs 0.218) |

City of London is worth flagging specifically: the smallest, sparsest
network (1,387 edges, 876 collisions total across five years, a
non-residential financial district with an atypical weekday-only traffic
pattern) is where XGBoost's temporal-split win is largest by far, and is
also where the GAT's own `gat_no_graph` ablation (0.320) beats the full
graph model (0.182) — the one borough where adding the graph structure
appears to actively hurt rather than help on the temporal split, plausibly
because there simply isn't enough data for the extra graph-attention
parameters to learn useful structure rather than noise.

**Answer to H1**: **partially supported, and more precisely characterised
than before.** There is no evidence the GAT reliably beats XGBoost on the
simple temporal (same-segment, next-year) task — XGBoost wins or ties in
6/7 boroughs. But on the spatiotemporal task (unseen segment, unseen
year) — the harder, more policy-relevant generalisation test — the GAT
shows a real, moderate, and now well-replicated advantage in 5/7
boroughs. **The honest, final answer is not "graph structure helps" or
"graph structure doesn't help" but "graph structure specifically helps
generalisation to roads without collision history, and does not
reliably help forecasting on roads that already have it."**

## 3. RQ: do conformal intervals achieve their target coverage?

| borough | model | method | target | empirical coverage | mean width | n |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Westminster | xgboost | MAPIE | 90% | 90.9% | 0.88 | 7,552 |
| Westminster | gat_temporal | manual split-conformal | 90% | 90.3% | 0.90 | 7,552 |
| Lambeth | xgboost | MAPIE | 90% | 91.2% | 0.66 | 8,238 |
| Lambeth | gat_temporal | manual split-conformal | 90% | **84.4%** | 0.59 | 8,238 |
| Camden | xgboost | MAPIE | 90% | 90.6% | 0.72 | 5,486 |
| Camden | gat_temporal | manual split-conformal | 90% | 89.3% | 0.73 | 5,486 |
| Kensington & Chelsea | xgboost | MAPIE | 90% | 91.0% | 0.83 | 4,581 |
| Kensington & Chelsea | gat_temporal | manual split-conformal | 90% | **85.5%** | 0.71 | 4,581 |
| Brent | xgboost | MAPIE | 90% | 90.9% | 0.41 | 9,007 |
| Brent | gat_temporal | manual split-conformal | 90% | 88.9% | 0.42 | 9,007 |
| Wandsworth | xgboost | MAPIE | 90% | 90.9% | 0.48 | 9,505 |
| Wandsworth | gat_temporal | manual split-conformal | 90% | 87.6% | 0.54 | 9,505 |
| City of London | xgboost | MAPIE | 90% | 91.7% | 0.72 | 1,387 |
| City of London | gat_temporal | manual split-conformal | 90% | 91.3% | 0.66 | 1,387 |

**Answer to H2**: **supported for XGBoost across every one of the seven
boroughs** (90.6-91.7% against a 90% target — tight and consistent).
**Only partially supported for the GAT**: at or above target on
Westminster and City of London, but under-covering in the other five, most
severely on Lambeth (84.4%) and Kensington & Chelsea (85.5%). This is a
real, honestly-reported limitation of the current manual split-conformal
implementation for the GAT — plausibly because a single calibration year
(2023) is a thin, possibly non-exchangeable basis for a split-conformal
guarantee — and a concrete direction for future work (a larger/rolling
calibration window, or an asymmetric CQR-style interval) rather than an
unexplained anomaly.

## 5. Scaling proof

The pipeline runs on any registered borough via `python
scripts/run_pipeline.py <Borough>` with zero ingestion-code changes, and
has now actually been run — not just registered — on all seven of
Westminster's real neighbours: Lambeth, Camden, Kensington & Chelsea,
Brent, Wandsworth and City of London. Every GAT run trained on GPU (CUDA,
RTX 4060 Laptop GPU), each borough's full model ladder completing in under
two minutes end-to-end. See `docs/scaling_to_london.md` for the staged
plan onward (multi-borough combined training is the recommended next
stage, directly motivated by the findings below).

## 6. What these results do and do not support

- They **do** support that XGBoost reliably beats the naive baseline and
  generalises spatially and temporally, across all seven boroughs, and
  improves further with real exposure data.
- They **do** support that conformal calibration works well for XGBoost —
  90.6-91.7% against a 90% target in every one of the seven boroughs
  checked — and only partially (2/7 boroughs at/above target) for the
  GAT's manual conformal implementation.
- They **do** support a real, diagnosed, ablation-confirmed benefit from
  the graph-attention architecture fix specifically on the
  **spatiotemporal (unseen-segment) generalisation task** — replicated in
  5 of 7 independently-run boroughs, a genuinely consistent finding, not a
  one-borough coincidence.
- They **do not** support graph attention as a general improvement for the
  simpler temporal (same-segment, next-year) task — XGBoost matches or
  beats it in 6 of 7 boroughs, sometimes by a wide margin (City of
  London).
- They **do not** yet include statistical significance testing — with the
  walk-forward method needing a single training transition per borough
  (only 4 usable years of data each), formal significance testing across
  the seven boroughs (e.g. a paired test on the seven PR-AUC differences)
  is a natural, well-motivated next step, not yet done.
- **A methodological lesson worth stating plainly, twice over**: the
  original, dramatic-looking result was wrong (data leakage), and when
  extending to more boroughs a second stale-number mistake was caught only
  by re-running rather than trusting an existing log entry (see
  `docs/decision_log.md`'s "process note", 2026-08-31). Both times, the
  much more modest, honestly negative-leaning result is what survived
  scrutiny. This is the process working as it should, not a failure of the
  project — and the seven-borough scale of this pass is precisely what
  made the second mistake visible in the first place (a two-borough
  comparison alone would not have surfaced the Westminster/results.md
  discrepancy as urgently).

## 2026-09-03 — Long-horizon crash history: a significant, replicated improvement

The headline result changed today for the first time in the
UCL-comparison workstream.

**The finding**: this project's feature set had always capped crash
history at 30 days (`ROLLING_WINDOWS = (7, 14, 30)`). Adding
`collision_count_90d` and `collision_count_365d` — no new data source,
just a longer horizon on the signal already present — produced the
first statistically significant model-side gain of the investigation.

| Borough | Previous best | + long history | Δ | Gao et al. | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Westminster | 70.03% | **75.39%** | +5.36 | 68.98% | **beats by 6.41** |
| Tower Hamlets | 67.34% | **75.37%** | +8.03 | 72.24% | **beats by 3.13** |
| Lambeth | 63.59% | **70.29%** | +6.70 | 76.59% | below by 6.30 |
| **Pooled (n=18)** | **66.99%** | **73.69%** | **+6.70** | **72.60%** | **beats by 1.08** |

**Pooled paired t-test p=0.001337, Wilcoxon p=0.001579, 16/18 windows
won — replicated on all three boroughs.**

**On the point estimate this clears the project's primary success criterion**
(`docs/gap_closing_plan.md` §4: pooled AccHR@20 above 72.60%). **But the margin is
NOT statistically significant**: our pooled 95% CI is [69.71%, 77.66%], which contains
their 72.60% (one-sample p=0.5730; bootstrap P(ours>theirs)=71.8%). Every per-borough
comparison is likewise a tie. The honest claim is that this project **matches** the
benchmark, not that it beats it. The p=0.001337 figure is the new model vs this
project's OWN baseline - a real improvement - not a test against the paper.

Three qualifications belong with every statement of this result:
Lambeth still trails by 6.30 points, so this is **not** a clean sweep of
all three boroughs; the evaluation protocol is stricter than theirs
(multi-year walk-forward vs their within-2019 6:2:2 split), so the
comparison is not perfectly like-for-like; and the ~140× zero-inflation
discrepancy against their Table 7.2 remains unexplained.

**Westminster now exceeds the reference paper by 6.41 points**, up from
a 1.05-point margin.

### Why this was found, and why it works

It came from analysing the *target* rather than the model. Two measured
properties of the crash data motivated it:

- Crashes essentially never repeat at a location: 3,085 Lambeth
  collisions occupy 3,068 distinct coordinates; only 1.1% share a
  coordinate with any other crash across three years.
- Risk rankings drift year to year (LSOA-level Spearman: 0.80 for
  2022↔2023, 0.71 for 2023↔2024, 0.60 for 2022↔2024).

Both imply that a *stable* estimate of per-segment risk needs far more
than 30 days of history. Measured directly at LSOA granularity,
walk-forward on the same six evaluation windows: 30d → 35.74%,
90d → 42.10%, 365d → 46.08%, 730d → 46.00% (plateau). The ~+10-point
gain from horizon alone was predicted *before* the experiment was built.

### Why it succeeded where ~18 other experiments failed

Every previous feature experiment added a **new data source** (POI
20-class, weather, road class, socio-demographics) to a model already
short of training data — all null. This adds no new source; it extends
the horizon of the most important signal already present.

It is also the mechanical **opposite** of the three transformations that
measurably hurt: junction redistribution (p=0.0432 negative),
rank-blending (75.8% → 29.9%), and AADF name-propagation (−10.42
points). All three traded per-segment *sharpness* for coverage or
smoothness. A 365-day count remains fully segment-specific, so it
sharpens the risk estimate instead of blurring it — and AccHR@20 is a
within-day ranking metric that depends entirely on between-segment
distinctions.
